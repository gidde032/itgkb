## Summary

Phishing works by rushing you past your judgment. The defense is a short mental checklist: does
the sender actually match, is it manufacturing urgency, where does the link really go, and was I
expecting this? Any one tell is reason to slow down; several together is a clear phish.

## The tells

1. **Sender mismatch** — the display name says one thing but the actual address is a lookalike or
   an unrelated domain. Hover/expand the real address rather than trusting the name.
2. **False urgency and threat** — "your account will be closed," "act within 24 hours," unexpected
   invoices or gift-card requests. Urgency is the core manipulation.
3. **Deceptive links** — the visible text and the real destination differ. Hover a link to preview
   the actual URL; watch for lookalike domains and mismatched destinations.
4. **Unexpected attachments** — an invoice, "voicemail," or document you didn't ask for, often
   asking you to enable content or sign in to view it.
5. **Credential/prompt bait** — a login page reached from an email link, or a request to approve
   an MFA prompt you didn't start.

## What to do

1. Don't click, download, or reply. Verify through a known channel — go to the site directly or
   contact the supposed sender a way you already trust.
2. Report it and delete (see reporting a suspicious email).

## Notes / Edge Cases

- Targeted (spear) phishing can be well-written and personalized — the sender/URL and the
  unexpected *ask* are more reliable tells than spelling mistakes.
- Legitimate messages rarely need you to act in minutes; urgency plus a link is the pattern to
  distrust.
